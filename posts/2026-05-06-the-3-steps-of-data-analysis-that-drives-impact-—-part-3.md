Step 3 — RECOMMENDATIONS Where insight becomes direction.\
This is the hardest step. And the most valuable one. Steps 1 and 2 are internal — gathering, understanding, interpreting. Step 3 is external — bringing findings to the business and telling them what to do about it. This is where an analyst stops being a reporter and becomes a strategic partner.

What makes this step different: In Step 1 the question was: "What data do I have?" In Step 2 the question was: "What is this data telling me?" In Step 3 the question is: "What should the business do because of it?"

That last question requires something no tool can give you. It requires judgment, business sense and the courage to take a position.

What this stage actually involves:

1. Translating insight into action. An insight without a recommendation is an observation. The job at this stage is to bridge the gap — here's what was found, here's what it means, and here's specifically what is recommended. Not vague. Not "we should investigate further." Specific. Actionable. Clear.

This is where the analyst's real value shows up. And this is what separates a good analyst from a great one.

It requires the analyst to bring:

Business acumen — understanding what the company can realistically pursue, what fits the overall strategy, what the business can afford and what it cannot

Customer understanding — knowing who the user is, what motivates them, what friction they face and what would actually change their behavior

Product thinking — understanding what levers exist within the product, what is feasible to build or change and how different initiatives connect to each other

Domain knowledge — knowing the industry, the competitive landscape and the external forces that shape what's possible

Creative thinking — because the data points to the problem or opportunity, but the strategy that addresses it comes from the mind of the analyst who understands the full picture

This is the 360 degree thinking that Step 1 was building toward. The business understanding, the ecosystem knowledge, the benchmark awareness — all of it feeds into this moment. The analyst who did the groundwork in Step 1 and the deep reading in Step 2 is now positioned to propose strategies.

This is what Steps 2 and 3 are really about. This is the analyst's main contribution. And this is what AI cannot replace.

2. Presenting with storytelling The recommendation needs to be a narrative — not a bullet point list, not a dashboard, not a data dump.

The structure that works: — Here's what was found — Here's why it matters — Here's what is recommended — Here's what to expect — Here's how success gets measured

Every stakeholder — technical or non-technical — should be able to follow that story and know exactly what action to take.

What this stage is NOT:

It's not about being right. It's about being useful.

A technically perfect recommendation that nobody acts on has zero impact. A recommendation that drives a decision — even an imperfect one — creates real business value.

It's not about presenting everything that was found. Stakeholders don't need to see the full analytical journey. They need the conclusion, the recommendation and the expected outcome. The rest stays in the workbook.

And this is why the best analysts aren't just technical — they're strategic. They don't wait to be asked what to do. They come with the answer.
