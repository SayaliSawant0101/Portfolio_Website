### **Step 2 — INSIGHT** Where the real analysis begins.

The shift in mindset from Step 1 to Step 2 is significant.

Step 1 is about asking: "What data do I have?" Step 2 is about asking: "What is this data telling me?"

That sounds simple. It's not.

Data doesn't speak for itself. It needs to be interrogated, challenged and looked at from multiple angles before any conclusion can be drawn.

- - -

What this stage actually involves:

**1. Looking for patterns and trends** What is going up? What is going down? What changed and when exactly? Is this a gradual shift or a sudden one? Trends tell you the direction. The "when" often tells you the cause.

**2. Segmenting and slicing** Averages can be deeply misleading. The real story lives in the segments — by acquisition channel, by device, by user cohort, by geography, by purchase behavior. An overall neutral result can hide a massive win in one segment and a massive loss in another.

**3. Comparing against benchmarks** This is where Step 1 pays off. Because the business context and benchmarks were established upfront — numbers can now be put in context. A 15% drop means nothing without knowing what the baseline was. Is this unusual or expected? Seasonal or structural?

**4. Identifying anomalies** What doesn't fit the pattern? What's surprising? Anomalies are often the most valuable signals. They either reveal a problem that nobody knew existed or an opportunity that nobody has spotted yet.

**5. Forming hypotheses** This stage isn't just about describing what's visible. It's about proposing WHY it's happening. "Conversion dropped 15% on mobile — hypothesis: the new checkout page added friction for mobile users specifically." Now there's something to test.

- - -

The most important skill at this stage:

**Distinguishing a problem from an opportunity.**

This is where business sense matters more than any technical skill.

Not everything that looks bad is a problem worth solving. Not every drop in a metric needs immediate action. Sometimes what looks like a problem is actually pointing toward a bigger opportunity hiding underneath.

From my own experience — at one point acquisition was strong but retention was weak. A surface level reading would flag it as a retention problem. But looking deeper it was actually a growth opportunity — one that led to building a loyalty program business case that drove 5% repeat purchase lift.

That's the difference between reacting to data and reading it.

Anyone can spot a problem. Identifying the opportunity takes judgment.

- - -

**What a bad insight looks like vs a good one:**

❌ "Conversion rate dropped 15% this month." That's an observation. Not an insight.

✅ "Conversion rate dropped 15% this month — the drop started on the 14th, affects mobile users only, and correlates directly with the new checkout page launch. Desktop CVR is unchanged. Hypothesis: added payment steps are creating friction specifically on smaller screens."

The difference is one question: So what?

If "so what" can't be answered — it's not an insight yet. It's just a data point.

- - -

**The role of storytelling:**

Before presenting the story to anyone else — it needs to be understood internally first.

At this stage the work is reading, exploring, building the narrative before bringing it to stakeholders.

The best analysts treat data like a detective treats evidence. The first explanation is rarely the right one. The process is to keep asking why until something actually makes sense.

- - -

**What this stage is NOT:**

It's not about which tool gets used. SQL, Python, Tableau — those are instruments. The insight comes from the thinking behind the query, not the query itself.

It's not about having more data. More data doesn't give better insights. Better questions do.

- - -

Step 1 gave you the data. Step 2 is where analysts earn their seat at the table.

Not by presenting more numbers. But by understanding what the numbers are actually saying — and what they're hiding.
