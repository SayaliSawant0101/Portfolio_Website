Recently finished Moneyball by Michael Lewis — it's not a baseball book. It's a data book dressed up as baseball. Published in 2003. 

Before anyone was talking about data driven decisions. 

Before data science was a career. 

Before machine learning was a buzzword. 

And it already knew everything. 

Oakland A's. One of the poorest teams in baseball. Couldn't afford the players everyone else wanted. 

Every other team was doing the same thing — Sending scouts to watch players and decide based on: Who looks like a ballplayer. Who has the right body. Who has the right feel. 

The New York Yankees had $103M to spend doing exactly that. 

Oakland had $41M. Their General Manager Billy Beane couldn't play that game. 

So he asked a different question. 

Not — who looks like a great player? 

But — what does the data say actually wins games? 

His answer: On Base Percentage.

How often does a player reach base per plate appearance? 

Because the logic is simple — You can't score if you make outs. 

The team that makes the fewest outs wins. 

Everything else is just a story. 

What Beane did in 2000 — we have a name for it today. 

Today we call this approach sabermetrics — using statistical analysis to evaluate players beyond the traditional numbers everyone was already watching. 

He didn't just find a better metric. He defined the North Star Metric for winning baseball. 

He cut through vanity metrics — Home runs. Stolen bases. Batting average. Numbers that looked impressive but didn't directly win games. And found the one number that actually predicted the outcome. 

Today every data team does exactly this — Which number actually drives the result? Ignore everything that just looks good on a dashboard. 

Beane was doing product analytics before product analytics existed. 

While the Yankees were paying millions for players who looked like stars — Beane found players nobody wanted. 

Too old. Wrong body type. Didn't look the part. 

But their On Base Percentage numbers were quietly elite. 

He bought them cheap. Built a team on data nobody else was reading. 

End of season scoreboard: 

Yankees spent $103M on the players everyone agreed were great. Oakland spent $41M on the players nobody wanted — but whose numbers said otherwise. 

Same number of wins. 

Then something interesting happened. 

Oakland's approach started working — visibly, undeniably working. Other teams noticed. 

By 2004-2005 teams started copying the model. 

Hiring economists. Statisticians. Data analysts. By 2010 almost every MLB team had an analytics department. 

Today analytics completely runs baseball and even other sports. Scouts still exist — but data drives almost every decision. 

Eeveryone copied Beane's approach. But the question Beane asked stayed — and evolved into what we now call data strategy. Data analysis. "What do the numbers actually say?" 

That question walked out of baseball and into every industry. Finance. Healthcare. Marketing. Product. Tech. 

Anywhere decisions were made on gut instinct and tradition — data walked in and asked the same thing. And it's still asking.
